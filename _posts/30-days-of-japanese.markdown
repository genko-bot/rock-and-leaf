A month ago, I decided to start learning Japanese. With a background in language learning and teaching, I knew that consistency and good methodology would be key. I wasn’t expecting fluency overnight, but I wanted to make steady progress using tools that align with comprehensible input and immersive learning. Now, after 30 days, it’s time to reflect on what’s been working, what hasn’t, and how I can refine my approach moving forward.

## What I’ve Been Doing

Over the last month, I’ve used a mix of different resources and techniques to build my Japanese skills:

Comprehensible Input Videos: I’ve been watching YouTube videos designed for beginner learners, featuring slow, clear Japanese with subtitles.

Anki: I’ve used spaced repetition flashcards (inconsistently) to reinforce vocabulary and kanji recognition.

Duolingo: I’ve completed my daily lessons, focusing on sentence structure and basic vocabulary.

The Moe Way (Loosely): This methodology emphasizes immersion and reading rather than explicit grammar instruction. I’ve adopted aspects of it but not followed it strictly.

## What’s Working

1. Hiragana Recognition

I can now recognize most hiragana characters with ease, which has significantly helped me in reading beginner-level content. Seeing words in context rather than isolated has reinforced my learning.

2. Comprehensible Input Videos

These have been incredibly useful for hearing Japanese in natural contexts. Since they are designed to be understandable at my level, I’ve been able to follow along without feeling overwhelmed. I’ve noticed my ability to parse spoken Japanese has improved, even if I can’t always recall vocabulary quickly.

3. Anki for Kanji

While I only recognize about 30 kanji so far, using Anki has helped me retain them better than if I had tried to learn them passively. The spaced repetition ensures that I don’t forget what I’ve already learned.

## What’s Not Working

1. Lack of Katakana Practice

Despite making solid progress with hiragana and kanji, I’ve completely neglected katakana. This is a big gap in my learning, especially since katakana is essential for reading loanwords and many beginner-level texts.

2. Duolingo’s Limitations

While Duolingo is great for consistency and motivation, I’ve found that it doesn’t provide enough exposure to natural Japanese or meaningful sentence construction. It’s good for reinforcement but shouldn’t be my main tool.

3. Not Enough Output

I’ve focused almost entirely on input—listening and reading—but haven’t practiced speaking or writing at all. While input-based learning is effective, I know from experience that some output practice will help reinforce what I’ve learned.

## Next Steps

Prioritize Katakana: I need to dedicate a few days to solidifying my katakana knowledge so I don’t keep running into words I can’t read.

Keep Using Comprehensible Input: This is one of the most effective tools I’ve used, so I’ll continue watching videos and maybe branch into beginner-level podcasts.

Add More Contextual Reading: Since The Moe Way emphasizes immersion, I plan to start reading simple manga or graded readers to reinforce my kanji and vocabulary.

Incorporate Some Output: I don’t need to speak fluently yet, but I should start trying to form simple sentences and maybe even write short journal entries in Japanese.

Final Thoughts

Overall, I’m happy with my progress in just 30 days. I’ve built a solid foundation with hiragana and basic kanji, but I have clear areas to improve, especially katakana and active language use. By refining my approach and staying consistent, I’m excited to see where I’ll be in another month!
